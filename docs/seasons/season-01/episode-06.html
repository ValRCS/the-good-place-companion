What We Owe to Each Other

What do we owe someone who trusts us when keeping our promise could put us at risk?

Spoilers through S1E6 onlyEpisode companion
Read after watching Episode 6.

This article discusses only events through this episode. Philosophical interpretation is distinguished from verified on-screen references. Exact scene timestamps remain a research task.

Short synopsis

Michael asks Eleanor to help investigate recurring problems in the neighborhood. Eleanor has promised to assist, but a successful investigation could expose her own secret. Chidi introduces contractualism as she considers the obligations of trust and promises. Eleanor attempts to distract Michael with an enjoyable day, complicating matters as their friendship grows. Meanwhile, Chidi helps Jason navigate a difficult interaction with Tahani. Michael ultimately concludes that he is responsible for the neighborhood's troubles and says he must leave.

Detailed recap

The neighborhood's difficulties produce a new problem for Eleanor: Michael wants her help understanding what has gone wrong. She has made a promise, but following through may uncover information she is desperate to conceal. Her own safety and her duty toward someone who trusts her appear to conflict.

Chidi's discussion of contractualism gives Eleanor a different way to reason about the problem. Rather than calculating only the result she prefers, she must ask what principle would be fair to the people affected. Would a rule allowing someone to deceive a trusting friend whenever honesty became inconvenient be defensible to that friend?

Eleanor tries to redirect Michael's attention by spending an enjoyable day with him. The plan is strategic, but the interactions also create a more personal bond. This matters because Michael stops being merely a danger to manage and becomes someone to whom Eleanor may owe an honest response. At the same time, Chidi attempts to help Jason manage the social demands of being presented as Tahani's soulmate.

Michael eventually takes responsibility for the difficulties afflicting the neighborhood and announces that he must depart. His decision closes the episode with the question of how people should assess their own responsibility when others might bear the consequences.

Characters and developments

  • Eleanor: faces a conflict between a promise and the desire to protect herself.
  • Michael: seeks the cause of the disturbances and eventually blames himself.
  • Chidi: introduces contractualist reasoning and assists Jason.
  • Jason and Tahani: navigate the expectations surrounding their assigned relationship.

Ethical dilemmas

  1. What makes a promise morally binding when keeping it is personally risky?
  2. May one mislead a trusting person to avoid serious personal harm?
  3. Can a moral principle be justified to each person affected by it?

Philosophical analysis

The following connections explain philosophical ideas in relation to the story; they are not claims that each philosopher or term is mentioned in dialogue. For a general introduction, read What is ethics?.

Scanlonian contractualism

T. M. Scanlon's contractualism asks whether principles governing conduct could be reasonably rejected by people motivated to find principles others could not similarly reject. The point is mutual justifiability, not an agreement that people have actually signed.

A simplified “veto” story is useful for teaching only if one retains the word reasonably. People cannot invalidate a principle merely because they dislike it or prefer a more favorable outcome for themselves; their reasons must be assessed alongside others' claims.

Promises and trust

Eleanor's situation turns abstract contractualism into a problem about reliance. Michael's expectation of assistance creates a reason against misleading him, but a responsible analysis must also consider the burdens a principle would impose on the promisor. The episode illustrates competing reasons without establishing a complete contractualist verdict.

Contractualism versus utilitarianism

Utilitarian reasoning asks how the consequences of competing actions affect overall well-being. Scanlonian contractualism instead examines whether a governing principle can be justified to individuals who stand to be affected. A policy can be attractive in aggregate while still prompting serious objections from an individual asked to shoulder its costs.

The title and the philosophical book

What We Owe to Each Other is also the title of Scanlon's 1998 book (Harvard University Press). That bibliographic link is secure; the current scene logs have not yet confirmed whether a copy is visible or Scanlon is named in dialogue. The book's title must therefore not be confused with a verified visual prop.

Discussion questions

  1. Should Eleanor keep her promise even if helping Michael endangers her?
  2. Why is reasonable rejection different from each person having a simple veto?
  3. Could an action maximize happiness yet be unjustifiable to one affected person?
  4. How does friendship change what Eleanor owes Michael?
  5. Does Michael's decision to blame himself follow from the evidence he has?
