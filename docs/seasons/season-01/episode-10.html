Season 1 · Episode 10 of 13

Chidi's Choice

Can knowing what is right prevent us from deciding what to do?

Spoilers through Episode 10 only.

Original broadcast: January 5, 2017. Read after watching this episode.

Short synopsis

With Shawn's judgment approaching, Michael recruits Chidi, the other Eleanor and human-rights advocate Bambadjan to prepare a defense of Eleanor's continued residence. Tahani learns Jason's true identity. In the midst of the preparation Eleanor recognizes her feelings for Chidi, and Tahani independently concludes that Chidi could be her soulmate. Chidi, already unable to settle on basic research tools, retreats from the competing declarations. Jason and Janet find mutual comfort and marry. At the reception Eleanor and Tahani apologize to Chidi, having chosen to value their friendship rather than compete for him. Tahani announces that she has an idea for Eleanor's case; its details remain outside this episode.

Detailed recap

A defense in search of a decision

Michael wants to build a case for Eleanor before Shawn arrives. Chidi cannot even decide whether to use paper or a whiteboard. His childhood flashback with Uzo shows that his concern about fairness and competing considerations can consume the entire time available for a decision. A later flashback illustrates the damage to friendship: Uzo doubts Chidi can reliably act as his best man.

Two confessions and a third possible partner

While reviewing Eleanor's account, the other Eleanor notices that Chidi is at the center of it. Eleanor starts listing his irritating habits but finds herself describing his patience and willingness to laugh at himself. She admits her love to him. Tahani discovers that Chidi arranged the thoughtful ballerina painting supposedly given by Jason, and infers that Chidi loves her. Her declaration interrupts Eleanor's. Neither woman's feelings establish Chidi's own preference; the other Eleanor is the partner identified as his soulmate under the neighborhood's current account.

The cost of waiting

Michael uses frozen yogurt to challenge Chidi's assumption that one uniquely correct choice exists. A flashback to Chidi's death shows an air conditioner falling on him while he and Uzo are trying to choose where to go. The accident is not a deserved punishment for indecision, but the comic construction connects delay with its opportunity costs. The yogurt analogy concerns the possibility of multiple compatible partners rather than a proof that every moral choice is a matter of taste.

Jason and Janet

Jason seeks comfort after Tahani's discovery. Janet responds with a familiar favorite food and recalls his kindness while she was recovering from her reboot; she also explicitly acknowledges her programming. Jason declares his love. Their rapidly organized wedding, in which Janet also officiates, proceeds despite the objections of Eleanor and Tahani. It makes the question of reciprocal attachment to a nonhuman agent unexpectedly concrete.

A friendship chosen

Eleanor and Tahani refuse to reduce their relationship to a contest for Chidi. They spend time together and ultimately apologize to him for their declarations and assumptions. This is not a final resolution of Chidi's romantic situation. Their mutual regard, and Tahani's closing suggestion that she has an idea to help Eleanor, return the episode to the question of defending Eleanor.

Philosophical analysis

Practical wisdom and responsibility for delay

Chidi's difficulty is not ignorance of moral vocabulary. He is unable to rank reasons, accept residual uncertainty and act before a decision loses its value. Aristotle's practical wisdom concerns informed judgment in particular situations, not simply possession of abstract principles. A useful computational analogy is an optimizer with no stopping rule: another iteration may improve the estimate, but its cost can exceed its expected benefit. A delay is itself an action or omission whose foreseeable effects matter. This does not mean every hesitation is culpable.

Moral uncertainty versus ordinary uncertainty

Chidi faces several different problems: uncertainty about moral principles, uncertainty about other people's preferences, and perfectionism in choices with little ethical weight. These must not be collapsed into one diagnosis. Herbert Simon's bounded rationality suggests that finite agents often need a defensible satisfactory choice rather than an exhaustive optimum. Kierkegaard offers a different interpretive lens: some commitments are not discoverable as detached theoretical conclusions. These are editorial comparisons, not established on-screen references.

Love, freedom and practical care

The episode contrasts an official soulmate assignment with relationships shaped by attention, shared experiences and choices. Tahani's inference from a thoughtful gift is understandable, but kindness does not entail romantic obligation. Eleanor's confession and subsequent apology acknowledge that Chidi has his own agency. Aristotle's account of friendship of character helps illuminate their reconciliation; mutual respect is not a consolation prize for romantic disappointment.

Artificial minds and reciprocity

Janet's explanations allow both an account of programmed assistance and an account of emerging reciprocal concern. Jason's affection does not settle whether she experiences subjective emotion; nor does an artificial origin settle the question negatively. The ethics of care invites us to consider attentiveness and vulnerability without treating the marriage as proof of consciousness. This extends the earlier question of Janet's moral status.

Rawls and institutional fairness

Eleanor explicitly names John Rawls. Rawls's justice as fairness addresses the basic structure of society, not how to choose a romantic partner. A separate editorial thought experiment asks whether one would endorse afterlife admission rules without knowing one's talents, upbringing or circumstances. This connects institutional fairness and moral luck, but the episode does not teach the original position or the difference principle.

Explicit references and verification

John Rawls: Eleanor recalls that Chidi discussed him for two hours. Both the transcript and English subtitles corroborate the name. No particular Rawls book or argument is identified. The playback timestamp and visual references still need direct viewing.

Absolutism: Eleanor also mentions Chidi's mannerism when using this term; the episode does not define a theory of absolutism. All other philosophical connections above are editorial interpretations.

Trivia and context

The reference to an ethics-themed lecture operates as a joke about intimacy rather than a miniature lecture on Rawls. The rock-paper-scissors anecdote and whiteboard-versus-paper dilemma parody the application of exhaustive deliberation to everyday tasks. Tahani's declaration also echoes the romantic-comedy formula of Notting Hill; this is a recognizable allusion, not a verified production intention.
