Moral decision-making

How finite agents translate reasons into action.

Distinguish the uncertainty

Moral uncertainty concerns which evaluative principles are correct or how they apply. Factual uncertainty concerns what is true or likely. Preference uncertainty concerns what one or others want. Analysis paralysis can occur under any of these, and even with no serious moral stakes.

Bounded rationality and satisficing

Herbert Simon emphasizes the limits of information, time and computational capacity. Instead of exhaustive optimization, a decision-maker can search until a reasonable aspiration threshold is met. A satisficing rule does not remove obligations or authorize arbitrary choices where rights are at stake.

Practical wisdom and omission

Aristotelian practical wisdom is judgment about what a concrete situation requires. Continuing to deliberate may consume resources, miss deadlines and burden others. Inaction is not always wrong, but it does not automatically escape moral evaluation.

Commitment and a stopping rule

Kierkegaard's account of existential choice invites reflection on decisions that constitute a person's commitments rather than merely reveal pre-existing preferences. For a finite agent, a defensible process includes setting stakes, identifying constraints, obtaining proportionate evidence, choosing, and reviewing the outcome.

The deliberation loop

Example: a committee deciding between two adequate meeting times should not spend longer debating than the meeting itself. A decision about serious irreversible harm warrants much more deliberation. Proportionality is the key.
