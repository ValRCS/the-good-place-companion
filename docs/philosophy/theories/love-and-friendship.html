Love and friendship

Affection, care, mutual recognition and commitment.

Love and the soulmate idea

In Plato's Symposium, Aristophanes offers a myth of divided beings longing for reunion. This is one speech among several competing accounts of eros, not a single doctrine that Plato simply endorses. Modern soulmate language often assumes a uniquely predestined partner; another view holds that love develops through shared history and sustained commitment.

Friendship and the virtues

Aristotle distinguishes friendship based on usefulness, pleasure and appreciation of character in Nicomachean Ethics VIII–IX. These forms can overlap. A friendship of character involves mutual recognition and wishing the good of the other for their own sake, not just seeking their approval.

Care, autonomy and commitment

Care ethics emphasizes responsiveness to particular needs and dependency. Care does not imply a debt of romance: a generous action does not entitle the giver to affection. Commitment can be freely maintained rather than guaranteed by a classification or external authority.

Love and artificial agency

A system may reliably respond to another's distress or exhibit apparent attachment. Whether that behavior involves subjective feeling, agency or reciprocal commitment is a further question. Neither behavioral similarity alone nor artificial origin settles the issue. Distinguish the human partner's experience from the system's putative experience.
