# John Rawls

## Intellectual background

John Rawls (1921–2002), an American philosopher who taught at Harvard, revived the social-contract tradition in modern political philosophy. His central concern was how free and equal citizens could justify the institutions governing their shared life despite divergent values and unequal social circumstances.

## Central ideas

Justice as fairness proposes principles for the basic structure of society. In the original position, representatives of citizens choose principles under informational restrictions: they do not know their particular class, talents, social identity or conception of the good. They do retain general knowledge about society and human needs. The device models impartiality; it is not a historical meeting or a claim that people should ignore all individual differences in everyday decisions.

- An equal and adequate scheme of basic liberties for all, compatible with the same scheme for others.
- Social and economic inequalities must attach to positions open under fair equality of opportunity and work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged.
- The first principle has priority over the second; fair equality of opportunity has priority over the difference principle. Aggregate wealth cannot justify sacrificing basic liberties.

Rawls's theory differs from classical utilitarianism, which can permit sacrifices of some persons for larger aggregate gains. It also differs from Scanlon's contractualism, which asks whether principles governing interpersonal conduct can be reasonably rejected. Rawls focuses on public institutions, fair cooperation and citizens' status as equals. His Kantian inheritance concerns autonomy and the moral equality of persons, not a straightforward application of Kant's categorical imperative. Reflective equilibrium tests principles against considered judgments and adjusts both in search of coherence.
