# Moral luck

**In brief:** The problem that moral appraisal often seems affected by factors outside an agent's control, although it also seems unfair to judge people for what they cannot control.

## Core distinctions

Thomas Nagel's influential discussion distinguishes *resultant luck* (how an action happens to turn out), *circumstantial luck* (situations one encounters), *constitutive luck* (traits and temperament), and *causal luck* (antecedent causes of one's actions). Bernard Williams explored how outcomes and agent-regret complicate tidy accounts of justification.

Consider two equally negligent drivers, only one of whom happens to strike a pedestrian. Their intention and carelessness could be similar while the results differ radically. The central problem is whether and how their moral blame should differ.

## Intent, foreseeability, and consequences

- **Intention:** what an agent meant to do or knowingly risked.
- **Foreseeability:** which harms a reasonable agent could anticipate.
- **Outcome:** what in fact happened, including effects of circumstance.

Evaluating outcomes alone can overstate control; evaluating intention alone may ignore grave harms and relational duties. Distinguishing those dimensions is more informative than treating “luck” as an excuse.
